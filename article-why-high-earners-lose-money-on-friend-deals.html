Why high earners lose money on ‘friend deals’

Athletes, artists and creators are pitched deals every week, often by people they trust. Why so many go wrong, and how to protect yourself without losing your friends.

By the Daraj team · [Publication date]

Key takeaways
  • Most bad deals come from trusted people, not strangers.
  • The danger is missing structure: no plan, no contract, no reporting.
  • A simple rule, independent review first, protects both your money and your friendships.

When your income rises quickly, something else rises with it: the number of people with a plan for your money. A cousin wants to open a restaurant. A teammate knows a property project. A friend from the old neighbourhood has a brand idea that just needs a famous name and some capital. Most of these people are not dishonest. That is exactly what makes these deals so dangerous.

Why trust makes us skip the checks

With a stranger, you would ask for numbers, a plan and a contract. With a friend, asking feels like an insult. So the deal starts on a handshake, the money is transferred, and nobody writes down who owns what, who decides, or what happens if it fails. The problem is rarely bad intentions. It is missing structure.

The four ways friend deals usually fail

  • Nobody has run this kind of business before.
  • Your money is the only real capital, so you carry all the risk.
  • There is no written agreement on ownership, roles or exit.
  • You are too busy to follow the numbers, and nobody reports them.
The deal is not bad because it comes from a friend. It is bad when it skips the questions you would ask anyone else.

Seven questions before you sign any deal

QuestionRed flag if…
Who has run this kind of business before?Nobody involved has done it
How much is everyone else putting in?Only your money is at risk
What exactly will you own?Ownership is “we’ll sort it later”
Who makes decisions day to day?It is unclear, or only them
How will you see the numbers?No monthly report is planned
How is your name used?Your name is the main asset, for free
What happens if it fails or you want out?There is no exit clause

Your name is also an investment

For public figures, the risk is not only money. When a business carries your name and fails, or treats customers badly, the damage follows you. Before you lend your name, treat it like capital: decide what it is worth, what you get for it, and how you can take it back.

How to say yes safely, or no without losing a friend

The simplest solution is to make a rule that has nothing to do with the person: every opportunity goes through an independent review first. It is not a judgement of your friend; it is how you work. A good opportunity survives the questions. A weak one shows itself early, and the decision is no longer personal.

This is exactly the role of a private business manager: one trusted, independent team that checks every deal before you sign, so you can focus on your career.

Illustrative example

A well-known content creator was invited by two friends to fund a café carrying her name. Before signing, an independent review showed that neither friend had run a food business, the budget left no reserve, and the draft gave her name to the café permanently. The deal was restructured: an experienced operator joined, her name was licensed for a fixed period with a right to withdraw it, and monthly reporting was agreed. The friendship stayed intact.

Questions owners ask

Isn’t it rude to ask a friend for all this?

Not when it is your rule for every deal. Serious partners welcome the questions; they protect both sides.

I already invested in a friend’s business. Is it too late?

No. We can review the business, put proper agreements and reporting in place, and help you decide what to do next.
